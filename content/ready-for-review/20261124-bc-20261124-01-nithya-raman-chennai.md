## Full draft

*L.A.'s next mayor learned city government in Chennai, with records requests, survey sheets and a map of where the public toilets actually were.*

In early October 2010, a small group of young urban planners in Chennai put a number out in public: a city split into 10 zones of around 400,000 people each had only 600 public toilets. Their project, Transparent Chennai, had built the count from right-to-information requests and questionnaires, and its lead, Nithya Raman, told the Times of India that the toilets had "very little relationship to slums in the area, residents of which would be most likely to use them."

In Zone 4, the stretch from Villivakkam and Ayanavaram to Sembium and Perambur, the city corporation said there were 20 toilets and a records request turned up 40. Somya Sethuraman, one of the project's researchers, found the people using them there were 70% men, and in some places men had taken over the women's latrines because women wouldn't come.

Sixteen years later, Raman is about to run Los Angeles, and the job she's walking into is the one she started on in Chennai.

She was born in Thrissur, in Kerala, in 1981, and at 6 she and her mother moved to Lafayette, Louisiana, where her father had come to study engineering. "When we came to the US we had nothing," she told Vanity Fair, and the family later settled in Burlington, Massachusetts, where she grew up and, at 22, became a citizen.

At Harvard she studied social studies and met Vali Chandrasekaran, and after graduating she went to Delhi without much of a plan. Not long after she arrived, more than 100,000 people were evicted from slums along the Yamuna River. "It felt crazily large and unjust and I wanted to get involved," she told the Los Angeles Times. She went to work with slum dwellers' organizations, first trying to stop the evictions and then documenting the resettlement colonies and pushing for running water and sanitation, which is how she ended up studying urban planning at MIT.

She finished there in 2008, went back to India and in 2010 started Transparent Chennai at the Centre for Development Finance at IFMR. Besides the toilets, the team built a database of the city's bus routes and fought slum clearance policies, and by 2011 volunteers were walking the streets of Ward 176 with Google Earth printouts, survey sheets and measuring tape. Nine of the ward's 11 public toilets turned out to be unusable, and one damaged in the 2004 tsunami still hadn't been repaired. "Basically our goal is to create the data needed for holding these elected representatives accountable," she told The Hindu.

In Kalyanapuram, a slum of 1,000 families on the Buckingham Canal, the data got something fixed. People there used to carry their own water to the toilet complex at the entrance, because it had none, until Transparent Chennai got involved in 2012. When Mint visited the next year, it had pink and white tiles, iron doors that latched from the inside, running water in every cubicle and a cleaning every night, and Thenappan G., a 57-year-old coolie worker, was paying 2 rupees to use it.

In 2013 she left Chennai for Los Angeles to join Chandrasekaran, whom she had married in 2010 and who was writing for TV, with *30 Rock* and *Modern Family* among his credits. She got a job as a management trainee in the city administrative officer's office and wrote a 2015 report on how the city was handling a growing homeless crisis, where she saw money going to the fallout "without addressing why people were on the streets in the first place."

In 2017 she co-founded the SELAH Neighborhood Homeless Coalition, named for Silver Lake, Echo Park, Los Feliz, Atwater Village and Hollywood, and in 2020 she ran against Councilmember David Ryu and won 52.87% to 47.13%, the first challenger in 17 years to unseat a sitting L.A. City Council member. This February, hours before the filing deadline, she pulled her endorsement of Bass and filed to run against her.

At home in Silver Lake, Chandrasekaran walks their twins, Karna and Kaveri, to school every morning. One St. Patrick's Day, Raman pulled beer taps at Tom Bergin's in the Fairfax District, then made a beeline for the man in the corner quietly nursing his Guinness. "He takes away my blinders and reminds me what the city is looking for," she told the Times.

She beat Bass on Nov. 3, and Bass called her two days later to concede. Raman's inauguration is Sunday, Dec. 13, and her first day as mayor is the Monday after.
