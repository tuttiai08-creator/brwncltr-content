## Full draft

On Friday 4 September 2026, *The Cycle of Love* opens in Los Angeles. Laemmle’s listing, checked on Tuesday 1 September, puts the first West L.A. date at the theater the page also calls the Royal — 11523 Santa Monica Boulevard — and says showtimes begin that Friday. The New York platform opening has already happened. Deadline’s specialty preview on 28 August said the documentary opened that day at the Angelika Film Center; IndieWire, the day before, had Variance Films’ Dylan Marchetti starting the run there on the 28th.

The useful fact on the Laemmle page is not another festival recap. It is a venue announcement: “Real-life subjects Lotta and P.K. Mahanandia will participate in Q&As following the 7:25 p.m. shows on Friday, 9/4 and Saturday, 9/5 at the Royal.”

That is the peg. The bicycle is what the poster is selling. The older reporting, and the director’s own interview, put caste in the middle of the story. The people the film is about are scheduled to be in the room.

### The logline wants a bicycle epic

Laemmle reprints the same synopsis the U.S. and U.K. campaigns have been using. In 1977, it says, PK, “a 23-year-old Delhi street artist from a poor, ‘untouchable’ family,” picked up paintbrushes and a second-hand bicycle and set off on a “6000-mile cross-continent mission” to find Lotta. Dogwoof’s releasing page and Deadline’s 7 May Variance-deal story use the same 23 / 6,000-mile shape. HeyUGuys, on 9 July, put the U.K. and Ireland cinema date on 18 September 2026 and kept the ‘untouchable’ adjective in the marketing copy.

Variance is the U.S. distributor. Dogwoof is the U.K. and Ireland distributor. The director is Orlando von Einsiedel, a British documentarian whose official billing on the film’s site sits next to executive producer Priyanka Chopra Jonas. Grain Media, his U.K. company, announced the project in August 2023 under the working title *Cycling For Love*, as a Grain Media and Sweden’s Kalabati Pictures production. This is a U.K.-director prestige documentary about an Odisha-born artist and a Swedish woman. It is not a Bollywood release, and it is not “Priyanka’s film” except as an EP credit.

The sell, in the trade, is joy. IndieWire’s Anne Thompson, on 27 August, quoted Marchetti calling it “a beautiful romantic universal story.” Von Einsiedel, in the same piece, asked why documentaries can’t be “about happiness and joy” and said love is “something everybody can agree on.” Variety’s Alex Ritman interview — the official site pulls his “tale of unashamed joy” line — has the director saying Chopra Jonas “felt this story transcends borders and nationalities.”

None of that is a lie about the bicycle. It is a choice about what to put in the window.

### The reporting that does not fit on a poster

P.K. Mahanandia is not a generic “South Asian” subject. BBC’s Vikas Pandey, in a 2016 Delhi feature fetched for this packet, wrote that as a schoolboy in “a village in the eastern Indian state of Orissa” he “often faced discrimination from upper-caste students because he was a Dalit — considered to be at the bottom of India’s caste hierarchy.” CNN’s Sophie Eastaugh, on 11 February 2016, called him a poor art student from eastern India, “seen as an ‘untouchable’ in his country’s caste system,” and “a member of India’s ‘untouchable’ Dalit caste.” He told CNN: “I was below the dogs and cows.” He described being forced to sit outside the schoolroom.

National Geographic’s Simon Worrall, on 2 April 2017, put Mahanandia on the record about the village: central Orissa, “called Angul now,” on the Mahanadi river. “The caste system in India is organized racism,” he said. IndieWire’s theatrical piece names him a Dalit sketch artist. In the Variety interview, von Einsiedel said “the most powerful part of P.K.’s story when I interviewed him was that he carried a lot of trauma from growing up under the caste system,” and that the journey was how he “learn[ed] to love himself.”

The BBC’s video page on 21 August 2026 — an interview with Chopra Jonas and von Einsiedel before the New York premiere — is titled as a conversation about “love and caste inequality.” The page says the film “also explores the caste discrimination that he faced in India.” This draft does not invent lines from that video. The headline is enough to show that even the EP tour did not leave caste off the table.

Lotta von Schedvin is the name on the Laemmle Q&A banner and on the film’s cast list. The 2016–17 reporting calls her Charlotte Von Schedvin. BBC and CNN describe a Swedish traveler whose family, in those pieces, “comes from Swedish nobility,” who drove the hippie trail to Delhi in 22 days, and who went with him to Orissa. BBC has her recognizing the Konark temple she had only known as a picture. CNN dates her as 20 in 1975. Those are their names for her, not a fairy-tale caption. She is Swedish. He is from a village in what those sources call Orissa, now Odisha. The film is British-directed. Flattening that into one “South Asian love story” would drop the only reason this site would publish it.

### What the film says happened, and what the older pieces say

The campaigns agree they met in Delhi when she asked him to draw her portrait, that letters followed, and that in 1977 he left without airfare. BBC dates the meeting to a winter evening in 1975, in Connaught Place. Nat Geo, with both of them on the phone, dates it 17 December 1975. A 26 August Moveable Fest interview dates the meeting 1976. This piece follows BBC, CNN, and Nat Geo, and leaves Moveable Fest as a conflict, not a tie-breaker.

The number on the poster is the problem.

The film and the venue say 23 years old and 6,000 miles, by bicycle. CNN’s 2016 piece says he was 26 in 1975, and calls the ride a 3,600-kilometer journey — and, in the same article, says he “admits taking many lifts — even being gifted a train ticket.” BBC has him starting on 22 January 1977, cycling about 70 kilometers a day, reaching Europe on 28 May via Istanbul and Vienna, “and then travelled to Gothenburg by train.” Nat Geo’s headline is “more than 4,000 miles.” In that interview Mahanandia says, “I didn’t just travel by bicycle. I got rides with trucks.” BBC’s 2016 piece calls him “the 64-year-old.” Nat Geo has him saying his passport reads 5 December 1951 and that he later learned he was born in 1949.

This draft does not pick 23 or 26, and it does not pick 6,000 miles or 3,600 kilometers. It reports that the movie’s logline and the older first-person journalism do not match, and that the older journalism already included trains, trucks, and lifts. Mahanandia told the BBC he “was cycling for love, but never loved cycling.” That line survives the conflict. The mileage does not have to.

The ‘untouchable’ in the logline is the film’s word. BBC and CNN use Dalit. His own sentences, in CNN and Nat Geo, are about school, temples, and a system he called organized racism. Marketing language is not a caste history.

### The family is on the credit roll. Chopra Jonas is an EP.

Grain Media’s 2023 note named the couple’s children, Emelie and Karl Mahanandia von Schedvin, as producers alongside Chloe Leland, Harri Grace, and Kalabati’s Chris Silvestri. Von Einsiedel, on that page, said “my co-producers, Emelie and Karl Mahanandia von Schedvin, tracked me down at an event I was speaking at in Sweden and handed me a copy of their parents’ book.” Variety repeats that they are co-producers. Laemmle’s producer line lists Karl and Emelie with Grace and Leland.

CNN’s 2016 piece named the children Sid and Emelie. The film credits say Karl and Emelie. This packet does not merge those names.

Chopra Jonas is an executive producer. The official site bills her that way. Deadline’s May deal story hyphenates Chopra-Jonas. Variety has her saying she was “immediately drawn in,” that championing “stories that feel intimate yet universal” is “central to the work we choose to do,” and that Purple Pebble Pictures is in the company stack with Grain Media, Kalabati, BD4, Tudip Entertainment, AMPL, and Artemis Rising. Von Einsiedel told Ritman a producer showed her an early cut and that she already knew the story. That is an EP credit on a U.K. documentary, not authorship.

IndieWire describes how the film was shot: most of an extended road movie over seven weeks in India and Sweden, with India standing in for more than a dozen hippie-trail countries, and an actor, Chirag Lobo, playing young P.K. in unscripted encounters. Laemmle lists Chirag Benedict Lobo and Mina Dale in the cast. The mature P.K., Thompson wrote, narrates. That craft note matters only as a reminder that the bicycle on screen is a reconstruction. The Q&A is not.

### What New York already filed, and what L.A. has not

Deadline’s specialty column on 30 August reported Variance’s Angelika opening “to $17k, with full houses both morning and evening,” and said the film “expands to LA’s Laemmle’s Royal next week.” Box Office Mojo’s weekend page, fetched 1 September, lists an opening of $14,904 from one theater, release date 28 August, distributor Variance. Those two figures are not the same. This piece attributes each and does not average them. It also does not treat “full houses” as a census of the Angelika.

IndieWire had Marchetti talking about Los Angeles and San Francisco after New York, then another 20 to 30 markets in September and October, including Boston and Seattle. Laemmle’s own board now lists Seattle, Glendale, and Encino on 11 September. Dogwoof still has U.K. cinemas from 18 September. None of that is the Friday night in West L.A.

The venue announcement names Lotta and P.K. Mahanandia. It does not name von Einsiedel or Chopra Jonas for those 7:25 shows. IndieWire’s “surprise guests” were about New York. Do not import them to the Royal.

### What this is not

It is not a plot recap, and it is not a review. This packet did not score the film.

It is not a claim that the 6,000-mile bicycle is the settled historical record. BBC, CNN, and Nat Geo already complicated the transport and the age. The conflict stays in the piece.

It is not a claim about what the couple will say after the 7:25 shows. No one from this publication will be in that room unless a human editor later reports it. The fact is the booking.

It is not proof that “the diaspora” feels one way. No Instagram, X, TikTok, or Reddit threads were opened as evidence of mood. The official site’s “acts of connection” campaign is site copy, not a measured audience.

It is not a caste essay built from Wikipedia. Jati labels that did not appear in the fetched BBC, CNN, or Nat Geo pages are omitted. The word “Odia” is not printed on those pages; the geography they print is Orissa / Odisha. Dalit is.

The movie opening in Los Angeles on Friday is a U.K. director’s documentary about an Odisha-born Dalit artist and a Swedish woman, sold as a bicycle epic, with the real couple booked after two 7:25 p.m. shows. The hook is the bike. The story the older interviews keep returning to is caste, and what it did to a person who then left Delhi without a plane ticket. If you go, the people who lived it are the ones the theater says will still be there when the lights come up.
