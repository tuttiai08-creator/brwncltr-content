# BRWNCLTR style guide

Every BRWNCLTR draft gets written, fact-checked and scored against this file. It sits on top of `style/VOICE.md` and `VOICE_LESSONS.md`, and where they disagree, this file wins.

## How this guide changes

Every correction Aziz makes becomes a rule here with its reason. If a rule makes the writing worse, flag it to the editor instead of following it.

## Audience

We write for South Asian Millennials and Gen Z in the US, Canada and the UK. They already know the scene, so we never explain the culture to them or remind them the subject is brown. They're here because we found the story first and tell it better.

## Voice

BRWNCLTR is the Complex magazine of South Asian culture. Our own "Who Is Maneesh?" is the house benchmark, and Complex and Rolling Stone India cover stories set the register. Before a draft ships, ask whether CNN or Vogue would post it as is. If not, it isn't done.

The prose has to sound like a sharp culture writer talking to a friend, so it's confident, specific and funny when the joke is earned. It never sounds like a report, a fact-check memo or a press release. We keep identity framing out: no "as a South Asian," no "representation matters," and no quote whose only point is representation. The lens shows through specifics, like the Hindi film on at home or the Toronto crowd.

**Let the fact land the joke.** If a detail is funny or pointed, state it plainly and stop. Don't add a line that explains why it's funny or what it means. Before: "That's a tough sell in Seattle, where fans still haven't forgiven Pittsburgh." After: "In Seattle, Pittsburgh is still the team that beat the Seahawks in their first Super Bowl." Same with quotes: let an exchange like Khosla's "Learning!" land on its own. Why: explaining the joke reads like a bot hedging; readers get it. (Aziz, Nov 24 2026.)

## The rules

**1. No fact-check narration in copy.** The reader gets the fact, never the story of how we checked it.
- Before: "That is as far as this desk can take the title. It is not a seahawks.com sentence."
- After: "Officially, the team just says 'the Khosla family,' but the NFL's own memo to clubs names Neeru Khosla as control owner, the person actually running the Seahawks."

**2. Open on a person or a moment, not dates or logistics.** The first line puts the reader in a room with someone.
- Before: "Today is Tuesday 1 September 2026. On 7 August, Apple's catalog dated a three-track object by Naisha..."
- After: "Skrillex is on the credits and Ty Dolla $ign is on the first track, but Naisha wants one thing clear about *911*: 'It feels completely mine.'"

**3. Short attribution clauses, each outlet named once.** Say "he told The Nod" and keep moving, and leave the rest of the sourcing to the verification section.
- Before: "Singh asked him to play Hansen, and Pattinson, according to THR, went quiet and said the role wasn't for him."
- After: "Pattinson went silent when Singh pitched him Hansen, then agreed to produce instead, Singh told THR."

**4. No "it's not X, it's Y" and no "what this is not" sections.** Say what the thing is.
- Before: "Instead of ending on Dan's dream being dead, Singh said, the film ends on a siren's call asking him, 'Are you an actor?'"
- After: "The new ending calls back to him, Singh said: 'Are you an actor?'"

**5. One quote per beat, with no commentary restating it.** Pick the best line and let it land.
- Before: "'I was shocked they didn't tell me,' she said. 'I was shocked I was playing such a pivotal role.' She told Deadline it was 'such a delightful twist,' one she did not see coming, and she made a meal out of it."
- After: Keep the Vulture line, "I swear to God, I think I'm not reading properly," and cut everything after it.

**6. No credit lists.** Keep only the credits that move the story, and never run a cast list, track list or awards list in sentence form.
- Before: "The Hindi lyric on 'The Shadowy Light' is by Kausar Munir, who shares the writing credit with Albarn and Gruff Rhys, who also sings on it. Ajay Prasanna's bansuri and the sarods of Amaan Ali Bangash and Ayaan Ali Bangash fill out the track."
- After: "Kausar Munir wrote the Hindi lyric, and Gruff Rhys also sings on the track, which carries bansuri and two sarods."

**7. Attribute a company's own claims to the company.** A network's ratings boast is the network's claim, not our fact.
- Before: "...and the season is averaging 19.3 million viewers globally, putting it among the top five HBO series launches in the platform's history."
- After: "The finale drew 10.5 million viewers worldwide in three days, and HBO says the season is averaging 19.3 million, which puts it among its five biggest series launches."

**8. No generic openers or pun endings.** An open that could start any story and a close that winks at the reader both get cut.
- Before: "If you bought your first share of anything on your phone, it may well have been on Robinhood." And the close: "This time, the thing he built has to get off the ground first."
- After: "When Robinhood's app launched in March 2015, 800,000 people were already on the waitlist for the thing Baiju Bhatt co-founded." The close ends on a fact, the first satellite test.

**9. Vary sentence length.** Medium sentences carry the story, a long one can paint a scene, and a short one lands a point.
- Before: "She was shocked they hadn't told her. Shocked, she said, to be playing a part this pivotal. She told Deadline it was a delightful twist. She did not see it coming."
- After: "Nobody told her Zoe was the Manhunter. She found out alone, late at night, a gummy in, reading episode four: 'I swear to God, I think I'm not reading properly.'"

**10. Build the lede and spine on an angle the big outlets didn't run.** If THR, Variety and Deadline all led with the same scene, we lead with something else.
- Before: "The notes went into an iPhone, typed fast, in Robert Pattinson's bathroom." Every trade opened on that bathroom.
- After: "Ajon Singh got so fixated on *Dhoom* that he taught himself to play 'Dhoom Machale' on the piano."

**11. Flow is the top rule.** Each paragraph is one thought that moves forward, so join related facts with "and," "so," a colon or "which is how," and save short sentences for real punches. We don't allow strings of single-fact sentences, stacked "which" clauses, setup and payoff pairs ("isn't supposed to... It's supposed to"), canned kickers, or source tags in the prose unless the source is part of the story.
- Before: "Now he's running a company called Cowboy. In May, his startup Cowboy Space raised $275 million at a $2 billion valuation to build its own rocket. The top stage of that rocket isn't supposed to come back down. It's supposed to stay in orbit and work as a one-megawatt data center. The rocket hasn't flown yet. But it's a long way from a stock app."
- After: "Now he's building a rocket. His startup, Cowboy Space, raised $275 million in May at a $2 billion valuation, and the plan is for the rocket's upper stage to never come home: it stays in orbit and runs as a one-megawatt data center. Nothing has flown yet, which is a strange place to find the guy who made stock trading free."
- Before: "He followed his dad into physics at Stanford, which is where he met Vlad Tenev. The two of them started a pair of finance companies in New York, which TechCrunch identified as Celeris, an algorithmic trading startup, and Chronos Research, which sold software to big investment banks. Then, in 2013, they started Robinhood."
- After: "At Stanford he followed his dad into physics and met Vlad Tenev. In New York the two built Celeris, an algorithmic trading startup, and Chronos Research, which sold software to the big banks. In 2013 they started Robinhood."

## Banned AI tells

Cut these on sight. They come from our unslop baseline.
- Words like delve, crucial, pivotal, vibrant, showcase, testament, underscore, landscape, foster, iconic, rich tapestry, game-changer and making waves.
- "Serves as," "stands as" and "boasts" where "is" or "has" works.
- "Not just X, but Y," forced groups of three, and "from X to Y" when X and Y aren't on a real scale.
- Trailing "-ing" phrases that add no fact, like "highlighting her range."
- Vague sources like "experts say" or "fans believe."
- Em dashes and en dashes. Use a comma, a colon or a new sentence.
- Filler such as "it's worth noting," "in today's age," "only time will tell" and "in conclusion," and generic wrap-ups or morals.
- Curly quotes, decorative emoji, title-case subheads and bold on every name.

## Structure

The lede is one to three paragraphs that put us with a person in a moment and get the best quote near the top. The nut paragraph follows, saying why this matters now and what our take is. The spine is the angle from rule 10, and every section hangs off it, so résumé material shows up only when it explains the present. Subheads are short magazine section labels in sentence case, never puns or packet shorthand like "Next." The ending lands on an image, a quote or a concrete date, never a summary or a moral.

**Each paragraph picks up from the one before.** Write a feature that flows, not a stack of beats under subheads. Use subheads sparingly, only for a real shift in time or place, never to hold up a thin paragraph. No parallel setups like "X was where... Y was where...". Give a casual reader the stakes in the first paragraph, and end on a picture (a scene, an image, the parade) rather than on a point or a summary. Why: beats under subheads read choppy and robotic; Aziz's Khosla rewrite showed the flowing version reads like a magazine. (Aziz, Nov 24 2026.)

**Background reads as story, not a resume.** Fold birthplace, schooling, moves and money into scenes and turns in the story, in the order they matter, not a list of credentials up top. Before: "Raman was born in Thrissur, studied at Harvard and MIT, and worked in Delhi before moving to Los Angeles." After: the Chennai toilet survey first, then a flashback to the Kerala-born kid who moved to Lafayette at 6, when it explains why she does this work. Why: a resume run is the fastest way to lose a reader. (Aziz, Nov 24 2026.)

## Sourcing and facts

Every fact and quote in the copy has to be in the research packet. Verification, conflicting reports and corrections of other outlets all go in the file's verification section, never in the prose. A doubt about a fact gets one clean sentence in the copy, once. We never claim an original interview that didn't happen, and we never write "she told BRWNCLTR" or "she told me" unless we talked to her.

Interpretation has to read as ours. Never put "in his telling," "he says" or "she says" in front of a line the subject didn't actually say. Before: "In his telling, the bike was the easy part." After: "Next to that classroom, the bike was the easy part."

Background is fair game from any source we can cite: where they were born, where they studied, how they got to the U.S., how they made their money. It doesn't have to come from the subject's own words. An immigrant-to-fortune arc is fine when it's told with specifics, and so is a comparison to another South Asian figure when the parallel is real (Vinod Khosla and Shahid Khan as NFL owners). Still out: "first South Asian" claims we can't back, and any framing that turns the piece into a representation win. (Aziz, Nov 24 2026.)

## The publisher test

Answer each question yes or no. A no on 1, 3, 4 or 6 is fatal, and the draft goes back.
1. Does the lede put us with a person, in a room or a scene?
2. Is there at least one detail only someone present could notice?
3. If you strip the parentheticals and the process talk, is it still a story?
4. Does each quote earn its space, set up by observation rather than stacked?
5. Does each paragraph hand the reader a reason to keep going?
6. Does the writer have a point of view, rather than summarizing other outlets' interviews?
7. Do sentence length and stress vary?
8. Is the bio stretch anchored to the present, with no "what this is not"?

## Read-aloud check

Before opening a PR, the writer reads the full draft out loud, start to finish, and checks five things:
- Any sentence you wouldn't say to a friend gets rewritten.
- If three sentences in a row each carry one fact, join them.
- If a paragraph doesn't push to the next one, fix the transition or cut it.
- Count the outlet names, and make sure each one appears only once.
- Search for "this desk," "according to," "which," "not just" and dashes, and justify every hit.

## Scoring

The Content Editor scores four things out of 10: Voice, Accuracy and sourcing, Structure, and Originality. The overall score is a judgment call, not an average, and a draft goes to Aziz only at 8 or above. More than two choppy paragraphs caps Voice at 6, and a fatal no on the publisher test caps the overall at 6. Without an original quote or a detail from our own reporting, a draft tops out at 7 overall.
