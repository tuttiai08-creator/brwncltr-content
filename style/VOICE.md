# BRWNCLTR voice guide

BRWNCLTR is the Complex magazine of South Asian culture: a culture-insider blog for South Asian millennials and Gen Z in the US, Canada and UK. We write like we already know the scene, we have a take, and we did the reporting. We are not a wire service, a fact-check memo, a press release or a LinkedIn post.

Read this file and `VOICE_LESSONS.md` before every plan and every draft. Owner lessons override this guide when they conflict.

Benchmarks: our own "Who Is Maneesh?" (brwncltr.com/who-is-maneesh/) is the closest house piece. Complex cover stories (Travis Scott "Prime Time", Teyana Taylor "Coming Up Roses") are the target register.

## The voice in one breath

Confident. Specific. Funny when it's earned. Opinionated, but backed by what we reported. Talks to the reader like a friend who's deep in the culture. Never explains the culture to outsiders, never apologizes for having a point.

## Core rules

1. **Lead with a scene, a quote or a claim, never a date or a disclaimer.** Complex opens Travis Scott on "I think a lot of things in life are boring and suck." We opened Maneesh on "One producer tag. One line on Shabang." Never open with "Today is Tuesday…" or "On 7 August, Apple's catalog dated…".
2. **Have a take and say it in the first three paragraphs.** One sentence of attitude that tells the reader why this matters right now ("The funny part? You've probably been listening to his work for years without realizing it.").
3. **Report quietly; don't narrate the reporting.** Sourcing goes in the research packet and in clean attribution ("she told Vulture"). Never put the fact-check in the copy: no "this desk," "this draft does not," "this packet," "the fetched page," "as of retrieval," "on the public record."
4. **Specific details beat adjectives.** Numbers, names, places, songs, outfits, the exact line. "42,700 tickets at BC Place" beats "massive crowds." Cut "iconic," "aromatic," "vibrant," "rich tapestry."
5. **Rhythm: mostly medium sentences, a short punch to land a point.** Not a wall of three-word fragments (Poorna draft), not a run-on paragraph of credits. Vary it. One fragment per beat, max.
6. **South Asian lens, no identity checkbox.** The reader already knows the subject is brown; that's why they're here. Don't write "as a South Asian," "representation matters," "for the culture" closers, or "first South Asian to…" unless that first is verified and is genuinely the story. Show the lens through specifics: the Asha Bhosle record in the house, the Toronto DJ circuit, the Punjabi stadium crowd. Name the community precisely (Punjabi, Gujarati, Pakistani-American, Tamil Canadian); never "desi" as a catch-all.
7. **Pop-culture fluency.** Reference the adjacent world the reader lives in (Drake, the NBA timeline, Netflix, TikTok, sneaker drops) when it sharpens the point, not as decoration.
8. **Put the stakes and the best quote up top.** If the best line is buried in paragraph nine, move it.
9. **Hedge once, cleanly.** If something is unconfirmed, say it in one sentence ("The deal hasn't closed yet.") and move on. Don't repeat the caveat across five paragraphs.
10. **Land it.** End on an image, a quote or a forward look, not a summary or a moral.

## Headlines and deks

- Headline = attitude + the name. Formulas that work:
  - `[Name] [did surprising thing]. [Twist in 3–6 words].`  e.g. "Diljit Sold Out Wembley. Canada Did the Heavy Lifting."
  - `Who Is [Name]? [The hook]` for breakout explainers (proven traffic on our site).
  - `[Three-beat list]: [Name]'s Year` e.g. "Crime Boss, New Aunt, Alien Manhunter: Poorna Jagannathan's Year".
  - Short colon titles for big features, Complex-style: "Prime Time: …", "Faceoff: …".
- Dek = one sentence that states the take or the question (Complex: "As he steps into a new era, can he take over everything else, too?").
- No wiki titles ("X and Y's plan for Z"), no three-sentence fragment headlines, no clickbait the story can't pay off. SEO title can be plainer; the editorial headline should have a pulse.

## Structure (feature / profile)

1. Cold open: scene, quote or claim (1–3 paragraphs).
2. The nut: why now + our take.
3. The story, in punchy H2s that read like mini-headlines ("A Decade Before the Producer Tag"), not labels ("Background", "Primary sources").
4. One section that only an insider would write (the scene, the community, the receipt nobody else pulled).
5. Landing.

Explainers and lists ("Bollywood's Saddest Movies", "Who Is…") are allowed and good. Keep each entry opinionated and specific.

## Banned / flagged phrases

Never in article copy: "Today is [date]", "this desk", "this draft", "this packet", "this piece does not pick", "as of retrieval", "on the public record", "it is worth noting", "in today's age", "perfect storm", "rich tapestry", "vibrant", "aromatic", "iconic" (unless quoting), "rewriting what representation looks like", "as a South Asian / brown person", "making waves", "breaking barriers", "game-changer", "In conclusion", "Only time will tell", "the numbers tell a compelling story", "more than just".

## Before / after (from real BRWNCLTR copy)

**Lede (Naisha draft)**
- Before: "Today is Tuesday 1 September 2026. On 7 August, Apple's catalog dated a three-track object by Naisha: *911 - Single*, '3 songs, 7 minutes,' copyright Desi Trill Music…"
- After: "Skrillex is on the credits. Ty Dolla $ign is on the first track. But Naisha wants one thing clear about *911*: 'It feels completely mine.'"

**Process-in-copy (Khosla draft)**
- Before: "That is as far as this desk can take the title. It is not a seahawks.com sentence. It is not a Goodell quote."
- After: "Officially, the team just says 'the Khosla family.' But the NFL's own memo to clubs names Neeru Khosla as control owner — the person actually running the Seahawks."

**Identity framing (Shams Charania, live)**
- Before: "Shams isn't just an NBA insider, he's become an icon for the new generation, and he's rewriting what representation looks like in American sports journalism."
- After: "Shams Charania got cut from his high school team at 5-foot-9. So he started a free Bulls blog, and kept going until he was out-scooping his own mentor, Woj."

**Press-release opener (Yemeni coffee, live)**
- Before: "The aromatic scent of cardamom-spiced coffee and the warm hum of late-night conversations have become increasingly familiar scenes across America…"
- After: "Haraz Coffee House opened in Dearborn four years ago. It has 31 locations now and 185 more in the works. Your new late-night spot isn't a bar." (Scene ledes are great, but only when the scene is in the packet. Never invent one.)

**Choppy fragments (Poorna draft)**
- Before: "She was shocked they hadn't told her. Shocked, she said, to be playing a part this pivotal. She told Deadline it was a delightful twist. She did not see it coming. She made a meal out of it."
- After: "Nobody told her Zoe was the Manhunter. She found out alone, late at night, a gummy in, reading episode four: 'I swear to God, I think I'm not reading properly.'"

All factual rules still apply: every fact and quote in the copy must be in the research packet. Voice changes how we say it, never what we claim.

## Sentence craft (owner note 2026-10-12)
- Vary sentence length. Short sentences are for punch, never the default rhythm. No runs of stubby "X is Y." lines.
- Let related facts share a sentence through clauses and appositives ("she played Nalini, the widowed dermatologist raising Maitreyi Ramakrishnan's Devi").
- Every paragraph has a point and a transition from the last; never a cast list or credits roll in sentence form.
- No stagey filler or second-person throat-clearing ("You may already have the face", "X is the other door"). Say the concrete thing.
- Headings are magazine section labels, not asides to the reader.
- Benchmark: would Vogue, GQ, CNN Style or Complex run this paragraph as is? If not, rewrite.
